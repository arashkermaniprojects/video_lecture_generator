THEORY SECTION COMPONENTS
-------------------------
The collapsible theory section sits at the top of the page. It contains these
sub-components (top to bottom):

  1. "What is RLHF?" paragraph
     Defines RLHF as aligning LLM outputs with human preferences via a learned
     reward signal instead of raw likelihood.

  2. "Core Idea" key-concept box (green border)
     Two equations side by side:
       Traditional:  max log P(token | context)
       RLHF:         max E[R(response)] - beta * KL(pi || pi_ref)
     This is THE equation of the lecture. You will return to it in the Epilogue.

  3. "The Three Stages of RLHF" paragraphs
     Three numbered descriptions: SFT, Reward Model, PPO. Establishes the
     mental model before the Pipeline tab provides the visual.

  4. "Why Not Just Use More Supervised Data?" example box (blue border)
     Comparisons are easier for humans than writing perfect outputs.

  5. "The Reward Model (Bradley-Terry)" subsection
     Formulas: P(y1 > y2) = sigmoid(R(x,y1) - R(x,y2))
     Loss: L = -E[ log sigmoid(R(x, y_w) - R(x, y_l)) ]

  6. "KL Divergence & Reward Hacking" subsection + warning box (red border)
     Explains unconstrained optimisation failure. Warning: beta too small =
     hacking, beta too large = no learning.

  7. "DPO: A Simpler Alternative" subsection
     DPO loss formula and brief comparison.

  8. "RLHF vs DPO at a Glance" key-concept box (green border)
     One-line summaries of both pipelines.


TEACHING STEPS
--------------
1. Open the tool in a browser. The theory section is expanded by default.

2. Read/paraphrase component 1 ("What is RLHF?"). Frame the problem:
   "We trained a language model on internet text. It can predict the next token
   well, but can it follow instructions helpfully? Can it refuse harmful
   requests? Next-token prediction doesn't optimise for these things."

3. Show component 2 (Core Idea box). Read both equations aloud. Say:
   "Today we'll unpack every symbol in this equation. By the end of the
   lecture, you'll understand what R, beta, KL, pi, and pi_ref are, and why
   each one matters."

4. Briefly mention component 4 ("Why Not Just Use More Supervised Data?").
   Ask the class: "Why is it hard to write the 'perfect' response to an
   open-ended question?" Take 1-2 answers. Then read the example box.

5. Tell students: "We'll now walk through the three-stage pipeline. I'll show
   you each stage first as a diagram, then let you interact with it."

6. Click the "Collapse" button to hide the theory section. This reveals the
   main interactive area cleanly.
